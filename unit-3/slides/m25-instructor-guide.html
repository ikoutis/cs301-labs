9. What averaging guarantees for squared error

Let a=f_A(x), b=f_B(x), and m=(a+b)/2. Expand the two squares to get ((a-y)^2+(b-y)^2)/2 = (m-y)^2 + (a-b)^2/4. The second term is nonnegative, so average predictions cannot have larger squared error than the average of these two squared errors. Summing over the same validation observations gives the corresponding MSE identity. There is no guarantee against the best member. This identity concerns squared error, not classification accuracy.

Use the three presets to connect the algebra with the previous calculation. The point is how errors combine, not that arbitrary disagreement is beneficial: adding a bad member can increase the average member error.

33. Cross-entropy: score the student’s probabilities

Motivate the loss before doing the arithmetic: the teacher considers several answers plausible, and the student should learn how much support to give each one. Cross-entropy is one choice for training this probability match, not the only possible matching loss. For a class answer k, use the log-loss -ln(q_k): assigning high probability to that answer incurs little loss, while assigning almost zero incurs a large loss.

Imagine class answers drawn according to the teacher probabilities p. The student average log-loss would be sum_k p_k[-ln(q_k)]. This is cross-entropy, CE(p,q). The thought experiment explains the weighting; actual training computes the weighted sum directly and does not need to sample teacher answers. Over normalized student distributions, this score is minimized at q=p, so it gives us an objective for matching the whole probability vector. A restricted student model may only approximate that match.

Continue the separate two-class illustration with fixed p=(0.70,0.30), initially q=(0.95,0.05). Reveal Weight the penalties, then Add them: cross-entropy. The penalties are about 0.0513 and 2.9957; their weighted contributions are about 0.0359 and 0.8987, summing to 0.9346. Despite both models choosing class 1, assigning only 0.05 to class 0 is costly because the teacher assigns it weight 0.30.

The student slider updates all calculations at full precision. Natural logarithms are used; displayed arithmetic is rounded. Cross-entropy does not reach zero when this soft target is matched. 34. KL divergence: the extra loss from mismatch

Explain why the subtraction is useful: cross-entropy includes loss that remains even when the student perfectly copies the teacher. We want a mismatch score whose zero means the full probability distributions agree. Subtracting the best possible score for this teacher gives that interpretation.

Begin with the current student score and the score when q exactly equals p. The teacher remains p=(0.70,0.30). At q=(0.95,0.05), CE is about 0.9346; at q=p, it is about 0.6109. Ask why the exact-match score is positive: the teacher is uncertain, assigning less than probability 1 to each supported class, so an exact copy also incurs positive log-loss.

Name the fixed exact-match baseline the teacher entropy, H(p)=-sum_k p_k ln(p_k)=CE(p,p). Subtract that baseline to define Kullback-Leibler divergence from teacher to student: KL(p||q)=sum_k p_k ln(p_k/q_k)=CE(p,q)-H(p). It is the extra loss caused by mismatch. Because H(p) is the minimum CE over normalized q, the difference is nonnegative and equals zero exactly when the distributions match. In the initial example it is about 0.3238. Individual summands may be negative; the complete sum is nonnegative.

Finally reveal CE(p,q)=H(p)+KL(p||q). Because the teacher is fixed, H(p) is constant with respect to student parameters. Subtracting it changes the zero point but leaves every student gradient and the optimum unchanged: CE and this KL train the student with the same objective. CE is convenient to compute as the training loss, while KL gives a directly interpretable zero for mismatch; either can be minimized. Use Match teacher to show KL=0 while CE remains H(p).

The direction is teacher p to student q; KL is not a symmetric distance. Natural logarithms give nats. The next slide plots these now-motivated quantities, showing the constant vertical gap between the two curves.

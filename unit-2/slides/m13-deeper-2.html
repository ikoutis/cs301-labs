Two ways to solve a system

direct: eliminationiterative
what it doesa fixed recipe, row operations until triangulara guess, improved one step at a time
what it costsO(n³) dense; with fill-in, sparsea sweep costs the nonzeros; the number of sweeps varies
when it stopswhen the recipe endswhen the residual is small enough
what it keepsthe factors L and U, fill includedthe matrix and a few vectors
what it givesthe solution to round-offa solution to the residual you asked for
  • Both are elimination's rivals in the same game: count, and conditioning
Notes. Everything so far built the answer by a recipe: elimination, in a fixed number of operations, and sparse elimination paying for the fill it creates. The other family starts with a guess, any guess, and improves it by steps that are cheap, one row of the matrix at a time, until the residual, the thing we can measure, is as small as we asked. It never forms L and U; it needs the matrix and a couple of vectors. What it costs is the number of sweeps, and that, as the next slides show, depends on the conditioning.

One equation at a time

x←x−ai·x−biai·aiai
  • Row i is a line (a plane): move the guess to its nearest point, down the perpendicular
  • After the move, equation i holds exactly; the others, not yet
  • Then equation i + 1, and around again: Kaczmarz, 1937
Notes. Kaczmarz's step. Take one equation of the system, a line in the plane or a plane in space, and move the current guess to the nearest point of it, straight along the perpendicular: the formula subtracts the right multiple of the row from x. That equation now holds exactly, and the others have been disturbed a little. Take the next one. The cost of a step is one row: a dot product and an update, as many multiplications as the row has nonzeros.

The zigzag, at three angles

  • A right angle: two steps and done
  • A small angle: the zigzag creeps along the lines
Notes. The same start, the same twelve steps, three angles between the two lines. At a right angle the second projection lands on the crossing. At sixty degrees the zigzag closes in fast. At ten degrees each projection moves the point only a little along the nearly parallel lines, and twelve steps leave it far from the solution. The angle is the conditioning of the system seen from the iteration's side.

How fast: the angle decides

  angle  cos^2 of the angle  error after 2 steps  after 20  after 40
    90°               0.000              3.1e-16         0         0
    60°               0.250                 0.21   8.1e-07   7.7e-13
    10°               0.970                 0.42      0.32      0.23
  • Two lines: each cycle of two projections multiplies the error by cos²θ
  • Straight lines on a log axis: a fixed factor each cycle
  • Nearly parallel, large condition number: a factor near 1, thousands of steps
Notes. On a logarithmic axis the error falls along a straight line: each cycle multiplies it by the same factor, the square of the cosine of the angle for two lines. At ninety degrees the factor is zero, at sixty a quarter, at ten degrees 0.97: a thousand cycles to gain a factor of a billion. An ill-conditioned system is slow for the iteration exactly as it was fragile for elimination, and for the same reason.

On a sparse system: a sweep costs the nonzeros

the 40 x 40 grid: N = 1,600 unknowns, 7,840 nonzeros; one sweep is 7,840 multiply-adds, elimination in the natural order about 2,560,000
4 on the diagonal, the notebook's grid   residual after 240 sweeps 1.3e-02; not yet below 1e-6
5 on the diagonal                        residual after 240 sweeps 1.1e-08; below 1e-6 after 177 sweeps
  • One sweep over the rows: one multiply-add for each nonzero
  • The number of sweeps is the conditioning again
Notes. The grid system of Part 4, 1,600 unknowns, five nonzeros a row, solved by sweeps of Kaczmarz from a guess of zeros. A sweep touches every nonzero once, a few thousand multiplications, against the millions of elimination in the natural order. With 5 on the diagonal, a well-conditioned version, the residual falls steadily by the same factor each sweep and passes a millionth before two hundred sweeps. With the notebook's 4 on the diagonal the system is nearly singular, as its condition number said in Part 3's sense, and the residual creeps: cheap sweeps, but far too many of them. Iterative methods live or die by conditioning.

When to stop: the residual

stop when ‖Ax−b‖‖b‖≤tolerance
  • The residual is what we can compute; it is the stopping rule
  • The error is bounded by about the condition number times the residual
  • Ask for a smaller residual: more sweeps, the same cost each
Notes. An iteration has to decide when it is done, and the only thing it can measure is the residual: how far A x is from b. So the stopping rule is a tolerance on the residual, and the error that comes with it is the condition number's business, as in the second part of this meeting. A direct method has no such choice: it runs to the end and gives round-off accuracy, whether or not you needed it.

Picking the rows at random

  • In turn, the rows may be visited in a bad order for a long time
  • At random, in proportion to the row's size: expected error factor 1 − 1/κF² for each projection
  • Strohmer and Vershynin, 2009: the randomized Kaczmarz method
  • The same idea, rows at random, is how the large models of today are trained
Notes. Going through the rows in order can be unlucky: a long run of nearly parallel rows gains nothing. Picking each row at random, with probability proportional to its size, gives a guarantee: the expected error shrinks by a fixed factor at every projection, one minus one over the square of a condition number of A, the Frobenius one. That is Strohmer and Vershynin's randomized Kaczmarz of 2009, and it is a cousin of the stochastic gradient steps that train neural networks: one row, one example, at a time, at random.

A family of iterations

methodone stepwhere it is used
Kaczmarzproject onto one rowtomography, signal recovery, huge overdetermined systems
Jacobi, Gauss–Seidelsolve one equation for its own unknowngrids, the walk's system, as a smoother
conjugate gradientsa clever step along the residualsymmetric systems: scipy.sparse.linalg.cg
  • All of them: a sweep costs O(nnz); the sweeps count follows the conditioning
  • None of them creates fill: the matrix is only read
Notes. Kaczmarz is one of a family. Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel solve each equation for its own unknown and go around, the natural thing to do on the walk's system, where every equation says a node is the average of its neighbours. Conjugate gradients, the workhorse for symmetric systems, chooses each step cleverly and needs far fewer sweeps; scipy offers it on a sparse matrix. What they share is the shape: a sweep costs the nonzeros, the matrix is only read and never filled, and the number of sweeps is set by the conditioning.

Direct or iterative?

choosewhen
elimination, solvethe system is small or dense, or many right-hand sides share one matrix
sparse elimination, spsolvethe fill stays small, as on a grid with a good order
an iterationthe matrix is huge and sparse, a modest residual is enough, or the fill would not fit
  • Both answer the same question of this meeting: how many operations, and how well conditioned
Notes. The choice in one table. Elimination when the system is small enough or dense, and especially when one matrix serves many right-hand sides, since the factors are reused. Sparse elimination when a good order keeps the fill small. Iteration when the matrix is enormous and sparse, when a modest residual is all that is needed, or when the fill would not fit in memory at all. In every case the two questions of this meeting decide: how many operations, and how well conditioned.

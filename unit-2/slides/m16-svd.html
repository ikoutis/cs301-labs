The dimensionality of data

How many coordinates do we need to represent the data accurately?

Meeting 16 · Purpose

Why we do this

Large datasets can be expensive to store and process. The SVD lets us use fewer coordinates and measure the resulting approximation error.

Part 2 of 4 · Meeting 16

The singular value decomposition

X = U S VT expresses the data using perpendicular axes ordered by their variance.

Factoring X as U S VT The decomposition

  • X has n points and m coordinates. Each column has mean 0; here n ≥ m.
  • X = U S VT: U is n × m; S and VT are m × m. S is diagonal.
  • The rows of VT are unit axes v1, …, vm, at right angles.
  • S: the singular values σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ … ≥ 0. The spread along vk is σk / √n.
  • U S contains the points’ coordinates along these axes.
  • In NumPy: np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)

The axes in two dimensions The decomposition

  • The SVD of the 200 × 2 matrix gives two axes, v1 and v2, at right angles.
  • The standard deviation along axis k is σk / √n.
  • As the cloud narrows, σ2 decreases and σ1 / σ2 increases.
  • A large ratio means the cloud lies close to a line.

The axes in three dimensions The decomposition

  • The SVD of the 240 × 3 matrix gives three axes at right angles.
  • σ3 / σ1 compares the smallest and largest spreads.
  • Look along v3 to see the plane; look along v2 to see its thickness.
  • The SVD axes need not align with the original coordinate axes.

Part 3 of 4 · Meeting 16

Variance, by direction

Total variance is the sum of the variances along the SVD axes.

The total variance Variance, by direction

  • Centring puts the mean at 0.
  • ‖xi‖² = xi1² + xi2² + xi3²: point i's squared distance from the centre.
  • Total variance = (1/n) Σ ‖xi‖²: the average squared distance.
  • It is also the sum of C’s diagonal.
  • From the SVD, total variance is (σ1² + σ2² + σ3²) / n.

A projection onto a direction Variance, by direction

  • v is a unit direction vector: v1² + v2² = 1.
  • The scalar projection of x on v: x · v = x1v1 + x2v2. The projected point: (x · v) v.
  • X v contains the n scalar projections.
  • The variance along v: ‖X v‖² / n.
  • Here, variance is largest along v1 and smallest along v2.

Variance along each axis Variance, by direction

  • Pythagoras for perpendicular axes:
    ‖x‖² = (x·v1)² + (x·v2)² + (x·v3)².
  • Averaged over the points, total variance is σ1²/n + σ2²/n + σ3²/n.
  • Axis k’s variance is σk² / n.
  • For a thin cloud, the first two axes account for nearly all the variance.

Data with many coordinates Variance, by direction

  • Example matrices: 50,000 words × 300 features; 610 people × 1,297 movies.
  • The reduced SVD gives r = min(n, m) perpendicular axes and r singular values.
  • The variances σk² / n sum to the total variance.
  • If the variances decrease rapidly, a few axes capture most of the variation.
  • These axes are the principal directions used in principal component analysis (PCA).

Part 4 of 4 · Meeting 16

Applications

Approximate the data using the axes with the largest variances.

Projecting onto two axes Applications

  • The cloud has least variation along v3.
  • Each point moves along v3 to the plane of v1 and v2: x − (x·v3) v3.
  • The projected points have coordinates (x·v1, x·v2): the first two columns of U S.
  • We store n × 2 coordinates. The discarded variance is σ3² / n.

Storage and computation Applications

  • Keep k axes: X ≈ Uk Sk VkT, with Uk n × k, Sk k × k, VkT k × m.
  • Memory: n k + k + k m numbers instead of n m.
  • Computation: a product X v costs n m multiplications; with the factors, n k + k + k m.
  • Noise: if the leading axes contain most of the signal, discarding the other axes can reduce noise.

LoRA: a low-rank weight update Applications

  • Example: a 4,096 × 4,096 weight matrix W has 16.8 million entries.
  • Fine-tuning for a new task changes W to W + ΔW.
  • LoRA, low-rank adaptation (2021): ΔW = B A, with B 4,096 × 8 and A 8 × 4,096. 65,536 numbers, 256 times fewer.
  • Training updates A and B and keeps W fixed. ΔW has rank at most 8.

Meeting 16

SVD and approximation

The SVD describes variation and helps us choose fewer coordinates.

To remember

  1. For centred data, X = U S VT gives perpendicular axes ordered by variance.
  2. The variance along axis k is σk² / n. These contributions sum to the total variance.
  3. Keeping the leading axes reduces the number of coordinates. The discarded variances sum to the mean squared reconstruction error.

Elimination and Fill-in

How elimination creates nonzeros and how ordering affects memory use and running time.

Elimination creates nonzeros

A[i,j]−=factor·A[k,j]for every j≥k
  • Row updates can turn zeros into nonzeros: fill-in
  • The factors can be much denser than A
  • The elimination order affects the amount of fill-in
Notes. Elimination subtracts a multiple of row k from row i. When the multiplier is nonzero, an entry that was zero in row i can become nonzero. This is fill-in. Sparse elimination must store and process these new entries, so its cost depends on the factors as well as the original matrix.

On the graph of the matrix

nonzeros of the star matrix: 16;  after elimination, centre first: 36  centre last: 16
  • Symmetric A: off-diagonal nonzeros connect unknowns
  • Eliminating a node connects its remaining neighbours
  • Centre first: all leaves joined; centre last: no fill-in
Notes. For this symmetric matrix, an off-diagonal nonzero A[i, j] gives an edge between unknowns i and j. Eliminating k updates the rows of its neighbours, creating connections among them. Its remaining neighbours form a clique in the symbolic fill pattern. Special numerical cancellations can remove entries. In the six-node star, eliminating the centre first joins all five leaves, increasing the stored pattern from 16 to 36 entries. Eliminating the leaves first adds no edges. Ordering changes the fill-in without changing the system’s solution.

Fill-in and solve time

  160 x 160   25,600            COLAMD           2,036,562             0.053
  • Compare factor nonzeros and solve time for each order
  • spsolve applies a fill-reducing ordering by default
Notes. The table compares natural ordering with COLAMD on grids of sizes 80 and 160. Both the factor nonzeros and the measured time change with the order. Fewer factor entries usually mean less storage and work, though the precise timing also depends on the implementation. The notebook’s spsolve calls use a fill-reducing ordering by default.

Nested dissection, in one picture

  • Cut the grid by a separator; eliminate the two halves first, the separator last
  • No edge joins the halves, so eliminating one never fills the other
  • Cut each half again: for a grid, O(N log N) nonzeros, O(N^1.5) time
Notes. A separator of n nodes divides the grid into two halves with no edges between them. Eliminate each half first and the separator last. Eliminating within one half cannot create edges to the other. Apply the same procedure recursively within each half: nested dissection. For a two-dimensional grid with N unknowns, this gives O(N log N) factor nonzeros and O(N to the 1.5) arithmetic work, compared with O(N squared) work in natural order and O(N cubed) for dense elimination.

Ordering and the cost of sparse elimination

  • Sparse elimination can create many additional nonzeros
  • Eliminating a node connects its remaining neighbours
  • Fill-reducing orderings limit factor storage and arithmetic work
  • Nested dissection uses separators to control fill-in
Notes. Dense elimination has an O(N cubed) operation count. Sparse elimination depends on the nonzero pattern and the chosen order. The star and grid examples show why sparse solvers reorder unknowns before factoring the matrix.

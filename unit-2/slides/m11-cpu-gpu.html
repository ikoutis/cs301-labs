CPU and GPU

The two processors that carry out your products: what is inside each, how one product is divided on each, and why the data is copied.

Two processors

  • CPU: a few large cores. Each runs any program, one instruction after another, billions a second
  • GPU: thousands of small cores. The same instruction at the same moment, each core on its own numbers
  • Each processor computes with the numbers of its own memory
Notes. The CPU, the central processing unit, is the processor that runs the programs of a computer: the operating system, the browser, Python. The GPU, the graphics processing unit, is a second processor in the same computer, built to compute the colours of the pixels of a screen: one short computation repeated for millions of pixels that do not depend on each other. A GPU therefore has thousands of small cores that carry out the same instructions at the same moment, and a memory of its own. Each small square on the right is one of the 2,560 cores of the T4 that Colab lends.

Inside a CPU core

  • One instruction: take it, read its numbers, compute, store the result
  • Several numbers per instruction: 8 pairs at once, on some processors 16. NumPy's loops use them; Python's interpreter cannot
  • The cache: a small, fast memory of the core. Numbers that stay in it are read fast
  • Several cores: NumPy and PyTorch divide a large product among them
Notes. A core does one small step at a time. Three things make a CPU fast at the arithmetic of this course. One instruction of a modern core can add or multiply eight pairs of numbers at once, and on some processors sixteen; the loops inside NumPy and PyTorch are made of such instructions, and a loop written in Python cannot use them, because the interpreter handles one number at a time. The cache: reading a number from it is much faster than from the main memory, so a computation whose numbers stay in the cache is fast; meeting 12 measures this with the same additions on short arrays and on long ones. And several cores share a large product.

One product, divided

  • S = X @ W.T + b: n × K entries, each a sum of m products
  • No entry needs another: the work divides in any way among any number of cores
  • A CPU: each core takes a band of rows. A GPU: thousands of entries at the same moment
Notes. The scores of a layer are a matrix with n rows and K columns, and each entry is the score of one neuron on one point. Because no entry needs another, the work can be divided in any way among any number of cores: a CPU gives each of its few cores a band of rows; a GPU has enough cores to compute thousands of entries at the same moment. That is why the product is tens of times faster on the T4 at the real sizes of Act 2.

What cannot be divided

  • A step that needs the result of the step before it
  • Training a network: every step starts from the weights the last step produced
  • Inside one step the products divide among the cores; the steps themselves do not
Notes. A computation in which each step needs the result of the step before it cannot be divided. The training of a network is one: its weights are improved in many small steps, and every step starts from the weights that the step before it produced, so the steps must be done one after another. Inside one step, the products can be divided among the cores.

Two memories

  • A GPU computes only with numbers in its own memory
  • 1. .to("cuda"): copy. 2. compute. 3. .cpu(): copy the result back
  • The copies pass through the connection between the two memories, and take time
Notes. Step 1 copies the tensors to the memory of the GPU, step 2 is the computation, step 3 copies the result back when the program needs it in the main memory. The copies take time, and so does handing a computation to the GPU: the block of Act 2 measures the copy of one matrix of size 4,096 beside the product at that size.

When a GPU does not pay

  • Data used once: copied there and back for one short operation, the copies cost more than the operation
  • Data that does not fit: the tensors must fit the GPU's memory, 16 GB on the T4
  • Steps that depend on each other: thousands of cores do not help when the work is one step after another
Notes. Three cases. Data that is used once: if the numbers are copied to the GPU for one short operation and copied back, the copies cost more than the operation. Data that does not fit into the 16 GB of the T4. And steps that depend on each other, as in training, where the steps run one after another. For a large product that stays on the GPU, as in Act 2, the GPU pays, tens of times over.

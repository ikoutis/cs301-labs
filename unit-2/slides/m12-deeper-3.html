Fewer bits, less energy

  • Half the bytes: twice the numbers for each byte moved
  • A multiplication: 3.7 pJ in float32, 1.1 pJ in float16 (2014, 45 nm)
  • bfloat16: the range of float32 in 2 bytes; made for Google's TPUs
Notes. A float32 number has a sign, 8 bits of exponent that set its range, and 23 bits of fraction that set its digits. float16 keeps 5 and 10. bfloat16, designed by Google Brain for its TPUs, keeps all 8 bits of exponent and only 7 of fraction: the range of float32, about two decimal digits. Neural networks turn out to need the range far more than the digits (Wang and Kanwar, Google Cloud blog, 2019). Fewer bits help twice: twice the numbers for each byte moved, so more operations per byte, and smaller multipliers: in Horowitz's figures for a 45 nm chip, a 16-bit float multiplication costs 1.1 picojoules against 3.7 for 32 bits, and the blog says a bfloat16 multiplier is about eight times smaller in silicon than a float32 one.

A chip made for the product: the TPU

  • Google's Tensor Processing Unit, described in 2017
  • 65,536 multiply-add units in one square grid, for 8-bit numbers
  • 92 trillion operations a second; 28 MiB of memory on the chip
  • 15 to 30 times as fast as the GPU and CPU of its day; 30 to 80 times the operations per watt
Notes. Jouppi and colleagues, ISCA 2017: Google built a chip that does almost nothing but the product of a layer. Its matrix unit has 65,536 multiply-accumulate cells for 8-bit numbers, 92 trillion operations a second at its peak, and 28 mebibytes of memory on the chip itself, managed by the software. On Google's own neural networks it was on average 15 to 30 times as fast as the GPU and the CPU of the time, and did 30 to 80 times as many operations for each watt. The TPU devotes much of its hardware to matrix arithmetic for neural-network inference.

A systolic array

  • The weights stay in the cells; the numbers of X flow through
  • Each number read from memory once, used by a whole row of cells
  • Partial sums pass down the columns between adjacent cells
Notes. How such a matrix unit is laid out: a grid of multiply-add cells, each holding one weight. The coordinates of a point enter from the left, one row of cells each, and pass from cell to cell; every cell multiplies the number passing through by its weight and adds it to a running sum coming from above, which leaves the bottom of its column as the score of one neuron. A number fetched from memory is used by every cell of its row before it is dropped, so reuse across the row raises arithmetic intensity. The precise operations per byte also depend on precision, weight traffic, and output traffic. The heartbeat-like rhythm of the data gave it the name systolic.

One GPU is not enough

  • GPT-3: 3.14 × 1023 operations, on 10,000 GPUs
  • Split the rows of X; each GPU computes its rows of S
  • Training: the GPUs must exchange numbers at every step
Notes. The count of GPT-3's training, from A deeper look 2, would take a single T4 thousands of years. So the work is split. The simplest way is the one drawn: each GPU takes some of the rows of X, the points, and computes their rows of S with its own copy of W. Training adds a catch: after every step the GPUs have to combine what they learned, the gradients of the weights, and agree on one new W before the next step. Communication can limit the benefit of distributing the arithmetic.

The links between processors

  • Local memory and interconnects have different bandwidth limits
  • NVLink: a link built only to join GPUs; up to 256 H100s in one system
Notes. From the data sheets of the T4, the V100 and the H100. A GPU reads its own memory fastest: 300 GB a second on the T4, 3,350 on an H100. The PCIe and NVLink figures shown are aggregate bidirectional bandwidths; the memory figures use the data sheets’ memory-bandwidth convention. They should not be read as one-way copy rates. To join GPUs to each other, NVIDIA built a link of its own, NVLink: 300 GB a second for a V100, 900 for an H100, and its switch system joins up to 256 H100s. Between servers the network is narrower still. Each step away from the multipliers costs bandwidth, and a large training run is designed around that.

Moving a number costs more than computing with it

  • A read from DRAM: hundreds of multiplications' worth of energy
  • Specialized chips keep the numbers next to the multipliers
Notes. Horowitz's figures for a 45 nanometre chip, from 2014; the figures illustrate the large cost of data movement. Values and ratios depend on the hardware. A multiplication of two 32-bit floats costs 3.7 picojoules. Reading 64 bits from a small cache costs about three times that, from a large cache about thirty times, and from the main memory, DRAM, hundreds of times. The same paper finds that a general processor spends about 70 picojoules on the overhead of each instruction. Energy, like time, goes to moving numbers; this is why the TPU, the systolic array and the tensor core all keep their numbers close.

Software written for the chip

  • X @ W.T in NumPy calls a BLAS library; on a GPU, PyTorch calls cuBLAS
  • Rewritten for each processor: its blocks fit that chip's caches and registers
  • The same O(n³) loops, tens of thousands of times faster than three loops in Python
Notes. Production matrix multiplication usually uses optimized libraries. NumPy hands X @ W.T to a BLAS library, Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms, such as OpenBLAS or Intel's MKL; PyTorch on an NVIDIA GPU hands it to cuBLAS. Each is written again for each processor: the sizes of its blocks match that chip's caches and registers, its inner loops use that chip's vector instructions, the work is divided among its cores. The algorithm and its count are the ones of Recap 1; the rate went from about ten million operations a second in Python's loops to hundreds of billions on the laptop's CPU.

An algorithm written for the memory: FlashAttention

softmax(QKT)V
  • Attention, in language models: every word against every word
  • FlashAttention (2022): computed block by block in the GPU's on-chip memory
  • Same asymptotic arithmetic complexity; less memory traffic
Notes. The models that write text compare every word of a passage with every other one: the scores Q K transposed, a softmax of every row, a second product with V. Students know all three pieces from this unit. Written plainly, the n by n matrix of scores is stored in the GPU's memory and read back, and for long texts that is most of the time. FlashAttention (Dao and colleagues, 2022) computes the same exact result block by block in the fast memory on the chip, so the full matrix of scores is never stored: the same asymptotic arithmetic complexity, with fewer bytes transferred. The backward pass recomputes intermediates and performs extra arithmetic to avoid memory traffic. The paper reports a threefold GPT-2 training speedup for its benchmark.

Designed for each other

  • bfloat16: a number format designed with the chip that uses it
  • 2:4 sparsity: two of every four weights set to zero
  • Sparse tensor cores skip the zeros: twice the arithmetic a second
  • The H100's data sheet: 1,979 trillion 16-bit operations a second with sparsity, half without
Notes. The other direction: models and number formats shaped by the hardware. bfloat16 was designed together with Google's TPUs. NVIDIA's tensor cores since 2020 can skip zeros when exactly two of every four weights are zero: training methods then keep the accuracy of a network while setting half its weights to zero in that pattern, and the sparse tensor cores do twice the math a second (Mishra and colleagues, 2021). The H100's data sheet quotes its tensor figures with sparsity, twice its dense ones. The hardware, the number format and the model are designed together.

A whole wafer as one chip

  • A chip is a small rectangle cut from a disc of silicon, a wafer
  • Cerebras WSE-3: the whole wafer as one chip, 46,225 mm2
  • 4 trillion transistors, 900,000 cores
  • Its memory on the chip itself, beside the cores
Notes. An idea at the edge of what is built. Chips are made many at a time on a disc of silicon and cut apart; a GPU is one such rectangle. Cerebras keeps the whole wafer as one chip: 46,225 square millimetres, 4 trillion transistors, 900,000 cores, by the company's own product page, with its memory spread on the chip beside the cores, so that the numbers of a layer rarely have to leave it. Whether such designs win is open; they show how far the hardware will go to keep the numbers near the multipliers.

What to take away

  • The product of matrices is the workload; chips are now built around it
  • Data movement can dominate running time and energy
  • Algorithms, number formats and chips are designed together
Notes. The meeting began with three loops and a count. The count has not changed: O(nKm) for a layer, on every machine here. Everything else has: the interpreter, the order of reading, the caches and their sets, the processor, its links, the number of bits, the algorithm that schedules the work. Use the operation count to estimate arithmetic work, then examine memory traffic and the hardware that executes it.

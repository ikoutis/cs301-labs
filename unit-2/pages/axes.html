The axes of a cloud

A cloud of points spreads more in some directions than in others. The directions of largest and smallest spread are its axes. Their lengths give the rank and the condition number of the data matrix.

1. A line through the cloud

Each dot is one of the 1,297 movies with 20 or more ratings. The 610 users are split at random into two halves of 305. A movie's x is its mean rating from one half; its y is its mean rating from the other. Turn the line and watch the spread of the projections.

The line passes through the mean of the cloud. Orange segments: the perpendiculars from 120 of the points to the line. Orange ticks: the feet of all the perpendiculars. Hover a dot to read the movie.

The projection of a point on a direction u, a vector of length 1, is the dot product of the centred point with u. The centred point is the point minus the mean of the cloud. The variance of the projections is the sum of their squares divided by n, as in the notebook's np.var. We use this population-variance convention throughout; using n − 1 instead would scale both eigenvalues equally and leave the axes and their ratios unchanged. The first principal axis is the direction along which the projections have the largest variance. Here it is u = (0.71, 0.70), near the diagonal: along it, both halves rate a movie high or both rate it low.

2. Two axes, an ellipse

The second axis passes through the mean, perpendicular to the first. Along it the projections have the smallest variance. The singular value of an axis is the length of the vector of the projections of all the centred points on it.

Each axis is drawn to two standard deviations of the projections on it: 1.24 and 0.44 stars from the mean. The dashed ellipse has these semi-axes. A standard deviation is the singular value divided by √n, with n = 1,297.

Put the centred points in the rows of a matrix X: n rows, two columns. The covariance matrix is XᵀX / n. Its eigenvectors are the two axes. Its eigenvalues are the squared singular values of X divided by n: 0.386 and 0.048. The condition number of X is the ratio of its largest to its smallest singular value, 2.8. So the condition number of the covariance matrix, the ratio of its eigenvalues, is the square of the condition number of X: 8.1.

3. Flatten the cloud

The slider multiplies every point's coordinate along axis 2 by a factor between 1 and 0. The coordinate along axis 1 and the mean stay as they are. At 0 every point lies on axis 1.

As the cloud flattens, the columns x and y turn toward each other and the ratio of the axes grows. Two nearly parallel columns give a nearly singular matrix and a large condition number. It is the picture of the two nearly parallel lines of meeting 13 (Linear systems, Part 2): there the rows were nearly parallel, here the columns are. At 0 the column y is a multiple of the column x, and the rank drops by one, from 2 to 1.

4. In 19 dimensions

Now each movie has 19 coordinates, one per genre: 1 if the movie has the genre, 0 if not. Each column is standardized: minus its mean, divided by its standard deviation. The axes of this cloud are the eigenvectors of the 19 × 19 correlation matrix ZᵀZ / n, with n = 1,297 movies.

Each genre at its weights in component 1 (across) and component 2 (up) of the original 19-column matrix. This map stays fixed when Family is added; the spectrum at right updates. A component is an axis of the cloud; its weights are the coordinates of the unit vector along it. Hover a genre for its number of movies.

The eigenvalues of the correlation matrix are the variances along the principal axes in 19 dimensions. A zero eigenvalue means a direction with no spread: one column is a combination of the others. A tiny eigenvalue means nearly so. The ratio of the largest eigenvalue to the smallest is the condition number of the correlation matrix, 7.3 here. Its square root, 2.7, is the condition number of the standardized data matrix Z.

5. The map of 50 movies

The 50 most-rated movies, along the two longest axes of the cloud of all 1,297. Hover a point to read it.

colour by

Before PCA, each user coordinate is centred across the movies. These means are already zero up to round-off after user centring and zero-filling. Hover a point for its title, year, mean rating and number of ratings.

Each movie is a point with 610 coordinates, one per user: the rating minus that user's mean rating, 0 where the user did not rate the movie. The picture shows the two directions along which those 1,297 points are most spread. The first axis moves with the mean rating: over the 1,297 movies their correlation is 0.82. At its right end are Pulp Fiction; at its left end, Independence Day. The second axis has blockbusters at the top (Independence Day) and cult and art films at the bottom (Fargo). The axes come from the ratings alone; the genres only colour the points.

The data are the MovieLens ratings ml-latest-small (GroupLens Research, University of Minnesota; F. M. Harper and J. A. Konstan, 2015): 100,836 ratings by 610 users, in unit-2/movies, used under the dataset's licence, for research and not for commercial use. Only the movies with 20 or more ratings are kept. The axes are computed with np.linalg.svd and np.linalg.eigh. The other pages of meeting 14: Correlation, in pictures and Two products. See also: Linear systems, in pictures.

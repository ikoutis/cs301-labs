Linear systems, in pictures

An equation is a line or a plane; a solution is a point on all of them. What the solution looks like, how far it moves when the data moves a little, and what elimination does to the planes.

1. An equation is a line; the solution is where the lines cross

Every pair (x, y) that satisfies one equation lies on a line. A solution of the system satisfies both equations, so it is on both lines: the crossing. Move the numbers and watch the crossing move.

The window of the notebook: x and y from −1 to 5.

The system

2x + 1y = 5

1x − 1y = 1

Solution

x = 2, y = 1

determinant 2 · (−1) − 1 · 1 = −3

In matrix form the two rows of A are the two lines and np.linalg.solve(A, b) finds the crossing. When line 2 turns parallel to line 1 the determinant passes through zero: the crossing runs off to infinity, and for an instant there is no solution at all, or a whole line of them if the two lines coincide. NumPy answers Singular matrix in both cases.

2. Nearly parallel lines, and the condition number

Both systems have the solution (1, 1). The right-hand side of the second equation is changed by a thousandth, as a measurement error would change it. Watch where the crossing goes.

A right angle: x + y = 2 and x − y = 0.

Nearly parallel: x + y = 2 and x + 1.001 y = 2.001.

The nearly parallel system

condition number 4,002

the solution moved by 0

that is 0 times the change of 0.001

the right angle, for comparison: condition number 1, moved by 0

The crossing of two nearly parallel lines slides far along them when one line moves a little. The condition number, np.linalg.cond(A), is an upper bound on that amplification for relative changes: 1 for the right angle, about 4,000 for the notebook's pair, and the larger the closer the lines are to parallel. With about 16 digits in a float64 number, a condition number of 10k can cost up to k of the digits of the solution.

3. Three unknowns: three planes

With three unknowns each equation is a plane. The first two planes meet along a line; the third plane cuts that line at one point, contains it, or misses it. Drag the picture to turn it.

Blue: x + y + z = 6. Orange: x − y + 2z = 5. Green: the third equation, which the controls change. The black line is where the blue and the orange plane meet.

The third equation

The system

2x + 1y − 1z = 1

rank 3 · determinant −9

x = 1, y = 2, z = 3

As the green plane turns toward the line it keeps passing through the point, but the system becomes ill-conditioned, as in Part 2: lift the plane by a little and the point runs far along the line. When the green plane contains the line, the third equation is the sum of the first two, the rank is 2, and every point of the line is a solution. Lift the plane then, and nothing is on all three: no solution. NumPy says Singular matrix in both cases.

4. Elimination: the planes pivot, the point stays

Forward elimination subtracts a multiple of one equation from another. The new plane passes through the same line as the two old ones, so the solution never moves; only the planes do. Three steps make the system triangular.

The moving plane pivots about the black line it shares with the plane it is being combined with. The dot is the solution (1, 2, 3).

The augmented matrix

1116
1−125
21−11

The pivot of step 1 is the 1 in row 1, column 1. Subtracting row 1 from row 2 removes x from the second equation.

After the three steps the matrix is upper triangular: the last equation reads −3.5 z = −10.5, a horizontal plane, z = 3. Back substitution climbs: −2y + z = −1 gives y = 2, and x + y + z = 6 gives x = 1. The point was there all along; elimination only turned the planes so that they could be read off one at a time.

2. The residual we can measure; the error we cannot

We know A and b, and a solver hands us a candidate x̂. The residual, how far A @ x̂ is from b, we can compute and see. The error, how far x̂ is from the true solution, we cannot: if we had the true solution we would not be solving. The red band holds every x̂ whose residual is at most ε. How far such an x̂ can be from the solution is the question.

x + y = 2 and the second line at the chosen angle. Red: every x̂ whose residual is at most ε. Orange: a candidate x̂.

Part 3 of the notebook: 100 nearly identical equations, the condition number set by eps. The residual is measured; the error is known there only because the experiment planted the solution, b = A @ x_true.

A well-made solver always returns an x̂ with a tiny residual: x̂ solves, to round-off, a system within a hair of the one it was given. That is a certificate we can always check, and it is the only one we get. It does not say that x̂ is near the solution of our system: the two can disagree, and the condition number is what bounds the disagreement, error up to about the condition number times the residual, in relative terms. With sixteen digits in float64, a condition number of 10k can cost up to k of them. In practice we have the residual and the condition number; the error we estimate from those, and never see.

Zeros are neutral. In the product A @ v a zero entry adds 0 × vj = 0 to its row's sum: the dense product computes it anyway, once for every zero, and reads the zero from memory to do so. CSR never stores the zero, so it never reads it and never multiplies it. The same is true of elimination with the zeros it does not create, which is the deeper look's story: a sparse solver's cost follows the nonzeros it has to handle, not the size of the matrix.

4. Fill-in: eliminating a node joins its neighbours

In the matrix of a graph, a nonzero A[i, j] is an edge between unknowns i and j. Eliminating unknown k subtracts a multiple of its row from every neighbour's row, and afterwards every pair of its neighbours is joined: new nonzeros, fill-in, drawn in red. The order of elimination decides how many. Let an order run, or eliminate by hand by clicking the nodes.

Sparse elimination costs what the fill costs: every new nonzero is stored and worked on. The 60 × 60 grid of the deeper look has 24 times the nonzeros of A after elimination in the natural order, 10 times with COLAMD, SuperLU's default, and 6 times with a minimum-degree order. Nested dissection cuts the grid by a separator, eliminates the two halves first and the separator last, so that nothing in one half can ever fill the other, and cuts the halves again: O(N log N) nonzeros and O(N1.5) work on a grid, against O(N²) nonzeros in the natural order and O(N³) work for dense elimination.

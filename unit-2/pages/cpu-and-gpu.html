CPU and GPU

The two processors that carried out your products in the lab: what is inside each of them, how one product of matrices is divided on each, and why the data has to be copied.

1. Two processors

The CPU, the central processing unit, is the processor that runs the programs of a computer: the operating system, the browser, Python. It has a few cores. A core carries out the instructions of a program one after another, billions of them every second, and it can run any program.

The GPU, the graphics processing unit, is a second processor in the same computer. It was built to compute the colours of the pixels of a screen: one short computation, repeated for millions of pixels that do not depend on each other. A GPU therefore has thousands of small cores, which carry out the same instructions at the same moment, each on its own numbers. It also has a memory of its own.

A product of matrices has the form of the pixels of a picture: every entry of the result is computed in the same way, and no entry depends on another. For that reason the layers of neural networks are computed on GPUs.

2. Inside a CPU core

A core does one small step at a time: it takes the next instruction of the program, reads the numbers that the instruction needs, computes, and stores the result. Three things make a CPU fast at the arithmetic of this course.

  1. Instructions on several numbers. One instruction of a modern core can add or multiply 8 pairs of numbers at once, and on some processors 16. The loops inside NumPy and PyTorch are made of such instructions. A loop written in Python cannot use them: the interpreter handles one number at a time.
  2. The cache. A core has a small memory of its own, the cache. Reading a number from the cache is much faster than reading it from the main memory. A computation whose numbers stay in the cache is fast, and a computation that must fetch every number from the main memory waits for them. Step 17 of the lab measures this difference: the same additions, on short arrays and on long ones. Meeting 12 comes back to it.
  3. Several cores. NumPy and PyTorch divide a large product among the cores of the CPU.

3. One product, divided

The scores of a layer, S = X @ W.T + b, are a matrix with n rows and K columns. Each entry is the score of one unit on one point: a sum of m products. No entry needs another entry. The work can therefore be divided in any way among any number of cores.

What cannot be divided

A computation in which each step needs the result of the step before it. The descent of Unit I is one: every update starts from the weights that the update before it produced, so the updates must be done one after another. Inside one update, the products can be divided among the cores; the updates themselves cannot.

4. Two memories

A GPU computes only with numbers that are in its own memory. A computation on the GPU therefore has three steps.

The copies take time, and so does handing a computation to the GPU. Step 15 of the lab measures both: the product at seven sizes on each processor, and the copy of one matrix.

When a GPU does not pay

  • A small computation. Handing the work over costs more than the work itself. Look at the smallest sizes in your table of Step 15.
  • Data that is used once. If the numbers are copied to the GPU for one short operation and copied back, the copies cost more than the operation.
  • Data that does not fit. The tensors of a computation must fit into the memory of the GPU: 16 GB on the T4.
  • Steps that depend on each other. Thousands of cores do not help when the work has to be done one step after another.

The numbers of the NVIDIA T4 (2,560 cores, 16 GB of memory) are from NVIDIA's data sheet. The machines that Colab lends for free had 2 processor cores and about 12 GB of memory in October 2026; your notebook prints what your own session has.

What matrix operations cost

The number of additions and multiplications behind each operation of the lab, what the notation O( ) keeps and what it drops, and how a count becomes a predicted time.

The count for each operation

As in the lab, n is the number of points, m the number of coordinates of a point, and K the number of neurons of a layer. A neuron is a rule that converts a point into a score, with one weight for each coordinate and a bias. A point is a row of X, and a neuron is a row of W.

The last two rows cost nothing, whatever the size of the array. For a transpose or a reshape, NumPy keeps the block of numbers where it is and only changes how it reads the block: which number counts as row i and column j. PyTorch does the same.

What O( ) keeps, and what it drops

In the lab, the time of the three loops is close to

time ≈ (the time of one pass) × n × K × m.

The notation O(nKm), read "of the order of n K m", keeps the product of the sizes and drops the factor in front of it. What it keeps answers the question of how the time grows:

When every size is doubled
The count grows asExampleThe time is multiplied by
O(m)one neuron, one point2
O(nm)one neuron, n points4
O(nKm)K neurons, n points8

When n, m and K are all equal to one size, the product of a layer takes size × size × size multiplications: it is O(size³). This is why, in Act 2, the step from size 2,048 to size 4,096 multiplies the time of the CPU by about 8.

What O( ) drops is the time of one operation. Step 17 of the lab measures it in several ways of computing, as operations per second, and the results differ by factors of thousands. The count is the same in all of them. What differs is how the program is run (by the interpreter of Python, or by code made for the processor), where the numbers are (in the cache of the processor, or in the main memory), and which processor does the work (a few cores of a CPU, or the thousands of cores of a GPU). The page CPU and GPU describes the two processors.

From a count to a time

A count and a rate together predict a time before the computation is run:

time ≈ (number of operations) ÷ (operations per second).

The calculator applies this to a layer of K neurons on n points with m coordinates, which takes 2 × n × K × m operations: one multiplication and one addition for each pass of the three loops. The three rates in it are examples. Replace them with the rates that your group measured in Step 17.
